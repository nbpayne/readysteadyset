The production needed two distinct bedrooms---a present-day version and a 1970s flashback version---on a tight budget that ruled out two separate location hires. Our answer: one set of flats, two completely different looks.

## Building the primary bedroom

Ten flats gave us a full four-sided room -- rare in film sets, where a missing "fourth wall" is standard, but the director wanted to shoot from any angle without seeing the studio beyond. We configured eight flats into the perimeter and reserved two for a practical door and a window.

The art department spent half a day applying vintage floral wallpaper directly to the flat surfaces. The flats' face takes paste and paper beautifully -- they painted a base coat first, then applied the paper in strips. The result looked like a room that had been lived in for decades.

## Controlling time of day

The window flat became the production's secret weapon. On day one, a Skypanel outside the flat---positioned at a low angle through a diffusion frame---read as early morning sun raking across the bed. By afternoon, the same setup shifted warmer and came from higher, reading as 3pm.

The gaffer never moved the light. She just changed the angle and the gel.

## The flashback version

On day three, the art department stripped the wallpaper (it came off cleanly), painted the walls a flat cream, brought in different props and bedding, and shot the 1970s flashback in a room that felt entirely unrelated to the one we'd been in for two days.

Two locations. One hire. One delivery charge.

## What this would have cost otherwise

The producer estimated that finding and booking two period-appropriate bedroom locations in Sydney---negotiating access, paying fees, factoring in travel and the risk of weather or noise---would have cost three times the price of the flat hire and added two travel days to the schedule.
