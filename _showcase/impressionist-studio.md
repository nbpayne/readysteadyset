The creative director arrived with a mood board of Berthe Morisot interiors - rooms flooded with filtered natural light, pale walls, the sense of air and dust caught in a sunbeam. The challenge: recreate that quality of light in a studio, on a single shoot day, in a way that could shift between eight different looks without the set feeling repetitive.

## Designing for painterly light

The key to Impressionist interior light is the window - specifically, multiple windows spaced along a wall, each admitting soft, diffused daylight from a slightly different angle. We custom-cut three of our standard flats with large arched openings and fitted diffusion silk behind each one. Behind the silk, we positioned large softboxes on stands outside the flats.

The result was a wall of soft, even light with subtle directionality - exactly the quality the Impressionists were painting.

## Fourteen flats in a wide arc

We set fourteen flats in a gentle arc rather than a straight line. This gave the camera more angular options - shooting slightly along the wall rather than flat-on to it - and created the sense that the room extended beyond the frame. In photographs, it reads as a large, airy space. In reality, it occupied about a quarter of the studio floor.

The remaining studio space was used for the lighting rigs, the styling area, and a second, simpler flat configuration for close-up portrait shots.

## The colour

The creative director specified Pantone 2562 C - a pale, cool lilac - as a wash over a warm white base. We pre-painted all fourteen flats before delivery, applying the base first, then dry-brushing the lilac over the surface in long vertical strokes to create a slightly textured, hand-applied feel.

On camera with the warm light coming through the window flats, the lilac read as almost white in the highlights and deepened to a soft purple in the shadows - exactly the tonal behaviour you see in Morisot's wall paintings.

## Eight looks in a single day

Because lighting changes were a matter of adjusting the softboxes behind the window flats - not repositioning location equipment or waiting for clouds - the photographer was able to move through eight distinct lighting setups between 7am and 5pm. That's an editorial's worth of content from a single day's hire.
